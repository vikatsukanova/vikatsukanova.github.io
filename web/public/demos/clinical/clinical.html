Preface

I designed the dashboard from scratch, working closely with a cardiologist to shape the UX and UI around real clinical workflows. Alongside the cofounders and a machine learning engineer, I helped define the MVP, balancing clinical needs and design goals with what the backend and ML-driven insights could realistically support.

01 / The challenge

Bringing clinical information into a usable workspace

During an assessment, clinicians need to connect the patient’s history with current findings and previous assessments. The design challenge was to make this information easy to reference while keeping the task at hand clear.

Working closely with the cardiologist helped me understand the relationships between that information and the workflow it needed to support. Those discussions informed how I organized the dashboard and presented its actions.

At the same time, I worked with the founding and engineering team to narrow the MVP to its essentials. This meant aligning the experience we wanted to create with the data and capabilities available to support it.

02 / The workspace

Three connected views of the patient

I organized the dashboard into three areas, giving each type of information a clear place within the assessment workspace.

The structure is designed to help clinicians orient themselves: whose record they are reviewing, what has already been documented, and where to continue.

01 / Patient context

Always available

Patient details, history, risk factors, medications, and allergies remain available for reference.

02 / Assessment history

Drafts and published

Assessments are grouped by diagnosis, with clear distinctions between drafts and published assessments so clinicians can find previous work or pick up an unfinished assessment.

03 / Supporting results

Within reach

Examination findings and test results sit alongside the assessment, keeping relevant information within reach.

03 / The assessment journey

From documenting symptoms to reviewing the plan

To explore how the dashboard could support the full assessment task, I developed a proposed four-step flow: Symptoms, Findings, Plan, and Review.

The prototype allows clinicians to start an assessment, return to a draft, and revisit earlier steps. Patient context stays visible throughout, while each step focuses on a distinct part of the task.

04 / Interface decisions

A clear hierarchy for a detailed task

The interface needs to accommodate substantial information while making the current task easy to identify. I used consistent grouping, readable labels, and restrained color to establish a clear hierarchy.

Keep context available

Predictable reference

Patient information remains in a predictable location, providing a reference point as the assessment progresses.

Make the next action clear

Focused steps

Section headings and primary actions give each step a clear focus. Supporting details remain accessible without competing for the same attention.

Make progress visible

Status in words

Labels such as “Draft,” “Saved,” and “Needs details” communicate the state of the work and help clinicians understand where to continue.

Use color with purpose

Status ≠ clinical meaning

Blue accents indicate selection and interaction. Workflow status is distinguished from clinical meaning, with text labels providing context rather than relying on color alone.

05 / Consistency and accessibility

Familiar interactions throughout the experience

Consistent cards, controls, and navigation patterns connect the dashboard and assessment flow. Repeated elements behave and appear similarly, reducing the need to interpret a new interface at each step.

Readable labels, visible keyboard focus, and a logical navigation order support interaction throughout the prototype. Status is communicated through words as well as color.

These patterns also form a reusable component library to support consistency as the product develops.

06 / Outcome and next steps

From clinical workflow to a tangible product experience

The work translated discussions with a cardiologist into a dashboard designed around patient context, assessment history, and supporting results. Collaboration with the cofounders and machine learning engineer helped keep the MVP grounded in what the team could deliver.

The accompanying assessment prototype explores how that workspace could extend into a connected documentation and review flow.

The next step is to evaluate the experience with clinicians: how easily they can find relevant information, resume an assessment, and recognize missing details before completion. These observations would guide further changes to the sequence, information density, and status labels.
